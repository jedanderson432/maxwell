**The universe is full of matter. Information is what makes it matter.**

Most of us look at a thing and see only matter. These exercises train you to see both matter and information at the same time: an it-bit.

Information is as physical as energy. It is the difference between beach sand and a smartphone, between pencil lead and a diamond, between a pile of ordinary atoms and everyone you love.

Twenty-three 60-second practices, each labeled for how well established it is, for seeing the simple pattern running through matter, life, laws, and machines, and what is becoming possible now.

Look at the back of your hand: nothing is only matter. Every object you hold is an *it* (the physical stuff) and its *bits* (the exact arrangement of that stuff) at the same time, an it-bit.

Matter tells you what a thing is made of. Energy tells you how much it can do. Information tells you which arrangement, out of all the possible ones, a thing is actually in.

The same carbon atoms can be a diamond or a pencil tip. The same element, silicon, is in beach sand and in a processor. What differs is the pattern.

Practice one exercise each day for about sixty seconds. Each pairs one concrete physical action with the verified physics underneath and a single sentence to carry with you. Every claim is labeled below as measured, established theory, a calculation, or synthesis, continuing the framework of [jedanderson.org](https://www.jedanderson.org/): once you see the world at the informational floor, everything becomes simpler to understand, steer, and protect.

*How this book is drawn: every picture in the PDF is one bit deep. Each point is black or white, yes or no, with nothing in between. The highlighters are the only color: yellow for lines that speak to protecting the living world, blue for lines about the power of information.*

## I. What Information Is

*Exercises 1 to 4. Information is a pattern that survives a change of body. It lives in surprise. And every yes-or-no answer doubles what you can tell apart.*

### 1. Pick Up an “It-Bit”

*Sand and chip: the same 144 dots, twice. Nothing was added on the right except arrangement.*

**Do this.** Hold any ordinary object in your hand: a coffee mug, a phone, a leaf.

Ask yourself what separates a silicon microchip from a handful of beach sand. They contain the same element. The only difference is information: the specific arrangement of atoms out of trillions of possible arrangements.

As Jacob Bekenstein summarized, thermodynamic entropy and Shannon information are conceptually the same quantity: counting how many bits it takes to specify a physical state. You are never holding mere matter; you are holding an it-bit: ordinary atoms held in an extraordinary pattern.

> **Carry this.** Matter is cheap and abundant. Pattern is what makes the world precious, and pattern is what intelligence can read, preserve, and direct.

*Status: established (silicon in sand and in chips; entropy and information as one measure: Boltzmann, 1877; Shannon, 1948). “It-bit” is this document’s name for the idea.*

### 2. Everything You See Reduces to Yes and No

*The leaf from the cover, as a machine holds it: 2,880 yes-or-no answers and nothing else.*

**Do this.** Look at the most complex thing in front of you: a human face, a piece of music playing, a city skyline, a storm cloud.

In 1948, Claude Shannon showed that information has one irreducible unit: the bit, a single yes-or-no question that cuts uncertainty in half. Every color in that skyline, every note in that song, every statute in a lawbook, and every satellite reading of a watershed can be translated into ones and zeros.

At the surface, reality looks like a thousand separate worlds. At the informational floor, there is only one alphabet.

> **Carry this.** That is why a single AI system can now translate languages, model molecules, and track emissions simultaneously: once you step down to the bit, every domain speaks the same language.

*Status: established (Shannon, 1948). Smooth things like sound and color are coded to whatever precision you choose. The last sentence is synthesis.*

### 3. One Pattern, Many Bodies

*One wave, drawn thirteen ways: nerve, muscle, air, voltage, numbers, radio, light, radio, voltage, speaker, air, eardrum, nerve. The shape is the only thing that makes the whole trip.*

**Do this.** Say “hello” out loud. Picture it traveling through a phone to someone you love.

Your hello starts as electrical pulses in your nerves. It becomes moving muscle, then pressure waves in air, then a voltage in a microphone, then numbers in a chip, then radio waves, then flashes of light in glass. Then radio again, voltage again, a vibrating speaker, air again, a trembling eardrum, and pulses in another person’s nerves.

Twelve changes of body. Not one atom from your mouth made the trip. The energy was used up and resupplied at nearly every step. The only thing that arrived is the pattern.

That is what information is: the thing that stays the same when all the matter and all the energy carrying it are swapped out. It always needs some body. It never needs a particular one.

> **Carry this.** What you love in a voice or a song is not made of any particular stuff. It is a pattern that matter takes turns carrying.

*Status: established. This is the working basis of all communications engineering.*

### 4. Twenty Questions

*Eight possibilities. Three yes-or-no answers. Three halvings. One number left.*

**Do this.** Find a number with three breaths. Ask someone to think of a whole number from one to eight.

On your first breath, ask, “Is it in the upper half?” On the next breath, halve the remaining possibilities. Do it once more. You know the number. Three yes-or-no answers can distinguish eight possibilities; if the choices are equally likely, that is three bits. Now reverse the roles and feel what just happened: something known only inside another person’s head became known inside yours. Nothing mysterious had to cross the room. Three well-chosen distinctions were enough.

Now pick one object in the room. Imagine a friend finding it by asking only yes-or-no questions. Each answer is one bit. If every question cuts the remaining possibilities in half, 20 answers single out one thing in a million. 33 answers single out one person among all 8.3 billion people alive. 266 answers single out one atom among all the atoms in the observable universe (about 10⁸⁰).

A string of 266 yes-or-no answers fits in 34 bytes. That is shorter than this sentence.

This is the first secret of information: every bit you add doubles what you can tell apart. Adding is multiplying. Ten more bits is a thousand times the reach.

> **Carry this.** Nothing is too large or too far away to be pointed at exactly. A short string of bits can reach anything.

*Status: established mathematics (Shannon, 1948). The atom count is an estimate.*

## II. Scale and Leverage

*Exercises 5 to 9. Your mind sips through a straw. The planet is one reflex wide. Life is one long message. And a single well-aimed bit can steer a ton.*

### 5. Feel Your 10-Bit-Per-Second Straw

*10,000 dots. It would take 10,000 pages like this one to show the hundred million bits that arrive. The circled dot is the one that fits through your straw.*

**Do this.** Pause and take in the room for ten seconds.

According to Caltech neuroscientists Jieyu Zheng and Markus Meister, your senses are pulling in roughly one billion (10⁹) bits per second right now, yet your conscious thought and behavior run at about 10 bits per second. That is a 100-million-to-1 bottleneck. You are standing under a waterfall of reality, sipping through a cocktail straw.

Over eighty years, ten bits a second adds up to about two gigabytes. Everything you will ever consciously notice, decide, say, and do would fit on a thumb drive. Attention is the rarest thing you own.

> **Carry this.** Feel the humility of that, and then feel the exhilaration: information technology doesn’t replace your humanity, it widens the reach of your straw, letting a 10-bit-per-second mind finally see and steer a billion-bit-per-second world.

*Status: measured estimate (Zheng and Meister, 2024). Older estimates put the sensory figure nearer ten million bits per second. The two gigabytes is a calculation: 16 waking hours a day for 80 years. The straw itself stays ten bits wide: the limit is inside the brain, and the authors expect even a direct brain link to run at about the same rate. What technology widens is what the straw can reach. That last point is synthesis.*

### 6. Earth Is One Reflex Wide

*0.2 seconds: one lap of the planet, in glass. One human reflex.*

**Do this.** Watch for the next thing that moves, and tap the table the instant you see it.

Your tap came about a fifth of a second late. That delay is your reflex: the fastest a person can answer something seen.

In that same fifth of a second, light in a glass fiber travels 40,000 kilometers. That is once around the Earth. A question sent to the far side of the planet and answered comes back in the same 196 thousandths of a second. (That is pure travel time. Real networks add a little.)

For nearly all of history, news crossed the planet at the speed of wind, water, and feet. Now, for information, the whole Earth sits inside one human reflex. A sensor anywhere can inform a decision anywhere before you can blink. What is missing is no longer speed. It is the loop: something set up to sense, decide, and act.

For four billion years, the planet had no nervous system. Today, the entire biosphere fits inside a single sense-infer-decide-act loop, and you are alive at the exact moment humanity is wiring it up.

> **Carry this.** For information there is no far away on Earth anymore. Only connected and not yet connected.

*Status: the travel time is a calculation (light in fiber moves at about 204,000 km per second). The phrase “one reflex wide” comes from [“The Law of Larger Selves.”](/essays/law-of-larger-selves) The lines about the loop and the planet’s nervous system are synthesis.*

### 7. You Are a Four-Billion-Year-Old Message

*2,000 letters of A, C, G, and T. Your text is 6.2 billion: three million blocks like this one, in almost every cell. (The letters shown are for illustration.)*

**Do this.** Look at the back of your hand.

Almost every cell there holds a text: 6.2 billion letters in a four-letter alphabet, about 1.5 gigabytes. You received it from your parents, who received it from theirs, in a chain of copying that has not broken once in close to four billion years. Not one of your ancestors, back to the first cells, failed to pass it on.

The atoms were swapped out in every generation. The text went on. Some passages (the instructions for the machinery that builds proteins) are recognizably the same in every living thing, from bacteria to you. That makes them older than the Himalayas and older than the Atlantic Ocean.

It is still being copied. Your body makes about 3.8 million new cells every second, and each one needs its own copy. That is roughly 24 quadrillion letters a second, with about one mistake per billion letters or fewer.

> **Carry this.** You are the newest body of a message that has outlived every body it ever had.

*Status: established biology. The copying rate is a calculation from measured cell turnover (Sender and Milo, 2021), good to an order of magnitude. Fossils put life at 3.5 billion years or more; genetic dating puts it nearer 4.*

### 8. A Word Stops a Ton

*The word: “stop.” The car: 1.5 tons, a hundred billion times the energy of the word.*

**Do this.** Say the word “stop” quietly.

That word left your mouth as about five millionths of a joule of sound. Said to a driver, it can halt a 1.5-ton car at highway speed: more than 500,000 joules of motion. That is a hundred billion to one.

The word did not supply the stopping energy. The brakes did, by turning motion into heat. The word did something else. It chose which of two futures happened. One bit.

This is how information acts on the world. It does not push. It steers energy that is already there, like a switch, a rudder, a nerve, a gene, a thermostat. Two honest conditions apply: the energy must be available, and something must be built to listen (an ear, a brain, a brake). Where both hold, the cost of a bit has a floor (exercise 11) and what it decides has no ceiling.

> **Carry this.** A bit is worth the difference between the two futures it chooses between.

*Status: speech power is measured (about 10 millionths of a watt). The ratio is a calculation. The principle is established (feedback control; Sagawa and Ueda, 2008 to 2012).*

### 9. All of Humanity in One Glass Thread

*Bits per second, where each step is 1,000 times more: your conscious thought, 10. Your speech, 39. Your senses, 1 billion. Every human talking at once, 325 billion. One glass fiber, 2026 record, 450 trillion. Every bar is measured or calculated. Sources are at the end.*

**Do this.** Say one sentence aloud at normal speed. Feel its pace.

Whatever language you speak, that pace carries about 39 bits per second. It was measured in 17 languages. Fast languages pack less into each syllable, so they all land near the same rate. Every poem, constitution, treaty, and scientific breakthrough in human history was squeezed through that tiny 39-bit channel.

Every human alive, talking at once, would come to about 325 billion bits per second. In 2026 a single ordinary optical fiber, a strand of glass about as thin as a hair, carried 450 trillion bits per second. All of humanity talking at once would fit in that one thread about 1,400 times over. Pause and marvel at what our species built at 39 bits per second, and then realize what becomes possible now that human intention can couple to machines exchanging billions of bits per second.

Machines now carry, copy, and combine patterns at rates no body ever will. You will not out-carry them, and you do not need to. Exercise 8 says where a bit’s worth comes from: the futures it chooses between. When you type one clear sentence to an AI, a hundred or so of your bits steer trillions of machine operations.

> **Carry this.** The machines carry. You choose. Choose well.

*Status: both rates are measured (Coupé and colleagues, 2019; NICT, 2026). The comparison is a calculation. The last paragraph is synthesis: an argument, not a measurement.*

## III. Information Is Physical

*Exercises 10 to 14. Bits sit on the same ledger as heat. Forgetting warms your hand. Knowing can lift a weight. And all the order on Earth, every tree, is paid for by sunlight.*

### 10. A Glass of Water Is a Finite Number

*100,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000. The bits of hidden detail in one glass of water, written out. Enormous. Finite.*

**Do this.** Hold a glass of water.

For 160 years physics has measured, with thermometers, a quantity called entropy. Ludwig Boltzmann showed what it counts: the hidden arrangements, all the ways the molecules could be placed and moving without the glass looking any different. Shannon’s measure of information, found about 70 years later, is the same formula.

So entropy is a count of bits, and the exchange rate is fixed. One unit of entropy (a joule per kelvin) is about 10²³ bits.

Your glass holds about 10²⁶ bits of hidden detail. That many yes-or-no answers would say exactly which state every molecule is in. The number is enormous. It is also finite, and physics sets a ceiling above it (roughly 10⁴² bits) that no object of that size and energy can exceed.

The water in your hand is an it you can drink and a finite list of bits that says exactly which it. Both, at once. Whether the list is the deepest layer, whether the it comes from the bit, is the open question John Wheeler left us. He expected the answer to be “an idea so simple, so beautiful” that we would wonder how it could have been otherwise.

> **Carry this.** Everything you can hold is the thing itself and a finite list of yes-or-no answers. Simple underneath. Endless on the surface.

*Status: entropy as a count of bits is established (Boltzmann, 1877; Shannon, 1948; Jaynes, 1957). The 10²⁶ is a calculation from water’s measured entropy. The ceiling (Bekenstein, 1981) is accepted theory that has not been tested directly. “It from bit” is an open conjecture.*

### 11. Forgetting Makes Heat

*The one white square: the least heat one bit can cost. All 240 squares: the energy to break one carbon-hydrogen bond.*

**Do this.** Rest your hand on your phone or laptop. Feel the warmth.

In 1961 Rolf Landauer showed that information obeys the laws of heat. Erasing one bit must release at least a fixed, tiny amount of heat: about 3 zeptojoules at room temperature (a zeptojoule is a billionth of a trillionth of a joule). The reason is simple. Erasing squeezes two possible states into one, and physics does not let possibilities vanish. They get pushed out into the surroundings as heat.

A lab measured this floor in 2012. So bits and joules sit on the same ledger. The warmth in your hand is the price of computing, and today’s chips still pay at least thousands of times more than the floor. The floor is physics. The gap is engineering.

Now compare changing matter. Breaking a single carbon-hydrogen bond, the kind found in fuels, plastics, and living tissue, takes about 240 times that floor. At the floor, knowing is far cheaper than breaking.

> **Carry this.** Information is not a ghost. It has a price in heat, and that is exactly why it can push on the world.

*Status: measured (Landauer, 1961; Bérut and colleagues, 2012). The chip comparison is an order-of-magnitude estimate. The 240 is a calculation for one carbon-hydrogen bond at room temperature (the bond-bit ratio); other bonds give other numbers. The last sentence of the body is synthesis.*

### 12. Watch Information Do Physical Work

*Something knows where it is. Nothing pushes it.*

**Do this.** Look at something moving without a plan: steam curling off coffee, wind in the trees, traffic outside.

For a century, Maxwell’s demon was a thought experiment about whether pure knowledge could sort chaos into useful energy. In 2010, physicists in Japan built it in a lab, lifting a microscopic particle uphill using only real-time information about its random thermal jiggling, without pushing it directly.

Information is not passive commentary about the physical world; it is a physical lever that converts randomness into directed work.

> **Carry this.** Every sensor, model, and real-time feedback loop you build is a working engine that turns mere awareness into physical protection.

*Status: measured (Toyabe and colleagues, 2010). The lab equipment used far more energy than the particle gained. The last sentence is synthesis.*

### 13. The Sun Feeds You Order

*One packet of sunlight in. About 20 packets of heat out. Energy in equals energy out. What Earth keeps is the difference in order.*

**Do this.** Stand in daylight, or by a window. Feel it on your face.

Earth does not keep the Sun’s energy. It sends almost exactly as much back to space as it takes in. The gap is under half a percent. Otherwise the planet would simply keep heating.

What changes is the form. Sunlight arrives as concentrated light from one small, very hot patch of sky. It leaves as a gentle heat glow in every direction: about 20 weaker packets of light going out for every one that came in.

Concentrated in, spread out. Physicists call that spread entropy, and it is a count of missing information. The difference between how tidy the light arrives and how scattered it leaves is the allowance that pays for every bit of order made on Earth. Every leaf. Every heartbeat. Every thought. Every computation.

> **Carry this.** You do not live on energy. You pass energy along. You live, everything lives, on the order it arrives in.

*Status: established (Schrödinger, 1944; Earth’s energy budget is measured). The ratio is a calculation: the Sun’s surface at 5,772 kelvin over Earth’s emission temperature of 255 kelvin, about 22.*

### 14. A Tree Is Made of Air

*The willow, at planting: 5 pounds. Five years later: 169 pounds. The soil, at planting: 200 pounds. Five years later: 200 pounds, less two ounces. The tree gained 164 pounds. The soil lost two ounces.*

**Do this.** Put your hand on a tree, or on anything made of wood.

In the early 1600s Jan van Helmont planted a five-pound willow in 200 pounds of dried soil and gave it nothing but water. Five years later the tree weighed 169 pounds. The soil had lost about two ounces.

The tree did not come out of the ground. About half the dry weight of wood is carbon, and every atom of that carbon was pulled out of the air. Nearly all the rest is oxygen, also from the air, and hydrogen from water. Only a percent or two came from the soil. Van Helmont thought it was all water. He had not counted the air.

The seed supplied almost none of the matter. It supplied the instructions: about 750 million letters for an oak, under 200 megabytes. Sunlight supplied the order (exercise 13). Air and water supplied the atoms.

> **Carry this.** The matter was everywhere, free. The seed brought the pattern.

*Status: measured (van Helmont, published 1648; the carbon content of wood; the oak genome, Plomion and colleagues, 2018). The megabyte figure is a calculation at two bits per letter.*

## IV. How Information Behaves

*Exercises 15 to 18. It lives in surprise. It is a difference. It can stay true through noise. And it is never erased, only spread beyond reading.*

### 15. Read the Gaps

*Y_U C_N R__D TH_S S_NT_NC_ W_TH N_ TR__BL_. You read it anyway. Roughly three quarters of every sentence is predictable.*

**Do this.** Read this out loud: “Y_u c_n r__d th_s s_nt_nc_ w_th n_ tr__bl_.”

You filled in every missing letter because you could predict it. A letter you can predict tells you nothing new. Information is measured by surprise: the less expected a signal is, the more it carries.

Claude Shannon tested this on English in 1951. People can guess so much of what comes next that each letter carries only about 1 bit, out of a possible 4.75. Roughly three quarters of every sentence is predictable.

Predicting and understanding are the same muscle. To understand a pattern is to need fewer bits to describe it: one short law replaces a long list of facts. Today’s AI language models are built by playing Shannon’s guessing game, predicting the next piece of text, trillions of times.

> **Carry this.** Information lives in what you could not have guessed. To learn fast, go where you are surprised.

*Status: measured (Shannon, 1951). “Understanding is compression” is a standard result of information theory, stated loosely.*

### 16. Feel Your Clothes

*A signal that stays the same fades from the report. A change brings it back.*

**Do this.** Notice the feeling of your shirt on your shoulders. Then notice that a moment ago you felt nothing.

Your skin never stopped touching the cloth. Your nerves stopped reporting it. A touch that stays the same carries no news, so the sensors go quiet within seconds and fire again the moment something changes.

Your eyes do the same. An image held perfectly still on the back of the eye fades from view within seconds. That is one reason your eyes make tiny jumps all day long.

This is the root definition. Information is a difference. A signal that never changes carries zero bits. Everything you have ever known arrived as a change against a background.

> **Carry this.** No difference, no information. To learn about anything, make it differ: move it, compare it, change one thing and watch.

*Status: measured (sensory adaptation; stabilized-image experiments of the 1950s). “Zero bits” is Shannon’s definition.*

### 17. Write a Sloppy A

*Tilted. Shaky. Half finished. Still an A.*

**Do this.** Write the letter A badly. Tilted, shaky, half finished. Look at it. It is still an A.

A reader snaps your wobbly mark back to the nearest letter, and the wobble is thrown away. That is the whole secret of digital. When a pattern is built from a small set of clearly separated symbols, noise can be wiped off at every step, before it piles up. A photocopy of a photocopy fades. The thousandth copy of a text is identical to the first.

In 1948 Shannon proved the general law. Over any noisy channel, below a certain rate, a well-coded message can arrive with as few errors as you choose. Life found this first: heredity is written in four distinct letters, and proofread.

On November 18, 2026, Voyager 1 passed one light-day from Earth, 25.9 billion kilometers out. Its radio is about as strong as a refrigerator bulb. What reaches our dishes is less than a billionth of a billionth of a watt. Its messages still arrive readable.

> **Carry this.** You do not need a noise-free world to build reliable, life-protecting systems. With the right encoding and feedback, clarity wins inside the storm.

*Status: established (Shannon, 1948; von Neumann, 1956). Voyager’s distance is NASA’s figure. Its radio power (about 20 watts) is approximate, and the received signal is a calculation from it.*

### 18. Stir the Cream

*The swirl. Stirred. Spread beyond reading.*

**Do this.** Pour milk into coffee or tea and stir once. Watch the swirl disappear. (No cup handy: watch your breath vanish on a cold day.)

Your eyes say the pattern is gone. Physics says no. At the level of molecules, the laws of motion never erase anything. Every later state could, in principle, be run backward to the one before it.

The swirl was not destroyed. It was spread into the exact positions and motions of about 10²⁵ molecules, far beyond any reading. That is what disorder is: information that has moved out of our reach. It is also why time seems to run one way. Patterns spread into fine detail easily and almost never gather back.

Reality keeps its ledger down to the last quantum state. You are not acting inside a forgetful void; every choice, every molecule shifted, every word spoken writes permanently into the state of the cosmos.

> **Carry this.** Nothing you do is ever erased from the universe. It is only spread beyond reading. So read things early, while the pattern is still a few bits.

*Status: established principle (reversible laws of motion; in quantum theory this is called unitarity), confirmed in every experiment so far. Two open edges remain: black holes, where most physicists now expect information survives, and what happens during a quantum measurement. “Read things early” is synthesis.*

## V. What We Can Build Now

*Exercises 19 to 23. One machine can become any machine. Records already move matter. We inherit, and we add. What extinction takes is pattern. And the loop that protects every living body can now be built for the living world.*

### 19. Your Phone Is Every Machine

*Camera, map, calculator, clock, flashlight, tape recorder: one phone.*

**Do this.** Hold your phone. Name five machines it has replaced.

Camera. Map. Calculator. Clock. Flashlight. Tape recorder. For most of history, each task needed its own machine. In 1936 Alan Turing proved it does not have to. One simple machine, given the right instructions and enough time and memory, can do anything that any computing machine can do.

So the difference between a camera, a piano, and a map is not in the hardware. It is in the bits. And the hardware itself is mostly silicon, the main ingredient of beach sand, arranged into about twenty billion switches. The difference between the sand and the chip is information too.

This is why abilities now arrive so fast. A new ability no longer needs new matter. It needs new bits, and bits copy perfectly (exercise 17) and travel at the speed of light (exercise 6). AI is the same universal machine running a newly found pattern. And now you can ask for the bits in plain words.

> **Carry this.** You own a machine that can become any machine. What it becomes depends on the bits you ask for.

*Status: established mathematics (Turing, 1936). The switch count is approximate. “A new ability needs only new bits” is synthesis.*

### 20. Hold a Twenty

*$1: 4.1 cents of paper and ink. $20: 7.3 cents of paper and ink. Same paper, same ink, three cents apart. The other nineteen dollars are not in the bill.*

**Do this.** Take out a banknote. Or open your banking app and look at the number.

A twenty-dollar bill costs 7.3 cents to print. A one-dollar bill costs 4.1 cents. Same paper, same ink, three cents apart. The other nineteen dollars are not in the bill. They are in an agreement, held in millions of minds and ledgers.

About nine of every ten US dollars have no paper at all. They exist only as entries in bank computers.

Now look further. The line between your yard and your neighbor’s is not in the soil. Your name, your marriage, the country you are standing in, what a factory may release into a river: none of it is in the atoms. It is all records. And those records decide where concrete gets poured and which forests stay standing.

> **Carry this.** Most of what governs your life and life in general has no atoms of its own. That is why information is so much more powerful and important than matter.

**This, my friends, is why information is so important.**

*Status: printing costs and money totals are Federal Reserve figures (2025 and 2026). The rest is plain fact about records, arranged as synthesis.*

### 21. Speak Six Thousand Years

*An ox’s head, turned: the letter A.*

**Do this.** Say aloud: “My mother gave me my name.”

The words mother, me, and name have been passed from mouth to ear, parent to child, for about 6,000 years. That is some 240 generations, starting in a language nobody ever wrote down. The letter A began as a drawing of an ox’s head about 3,800 years ago. Zero, as a number, is about 1,400 years old.

Almost nothing you think with was made by you. This is your second inheritance. The first is the 1.5 gigabytes from your parents (exercise 7). The second is everything people before you worked out and managed to pass on: words, numbers, tools, laws, songs. It is why each generation starts where the last one stopped.

AI language models are trained on a large share of what humans have written, thousands of lifetimes of reading. When you ask one a question, you are consulting a compressed, imperfect copy of that inheritance.

> **Carry this.** You think with borrowed bits, hundreds of generations deep. Add something worth borrowing.

*Status: established historical linguistics and history of writing; dates are approximate. The reading comparison is an order-of-magnitude estimate.*

### 22. Nothing Was Lost but the Pattern

*A living pattern. The same dots, every one still here. 9,861 white dots on the left. The same 9,861 on the right. Nothing is missing but the arrangement.*

**Do this.** Look at the nearest living thing that is not human: a bird, a tree, a weed in a crack.

When it dies, every atom in it stays on Earth and is used again. When the last of its kind dies, the atoms still stay. Not one goes missing.

What goes is a text: millions to billions of letters, copied and corrected without a break for close to four billion years (exercise 7) and, for most species, written down nowhere else. Like the swirl in the cream (exercise 18), it is not erased from the universe. It is spread beyond any reading, and no one can gather it back.

So what we protect when we protect nature is not the atoms. It is the arrangement: the patterns that took the longest to write.

> **Carry this.** Extinction loses no atoms. It loses information. To protect nature is to protect patterns.

*Status: that matter is conserved is established. Genome sizes are measured. “Extinction is a loss of information” is synthesis: the old comparison to a burning library, stated in bits.*

### 23. Hold Your Breath

*Sense, decide, act. Every second, for a lifetime.*

**Do this.** Breathe out, then hold until you feel the first urge to breathe. Then breathe.

That urge was not a lack of oxygen. You had plenty. It was a sensor at the base of your brain detecting carbon dioxide building up in your blood, and ordering your chest to act.

Your body runs this loop every second of your life: sense, decide, act. It holds its own carbon dioxide within a narrow band, with no annual report and no inspection. It does not let the problem spread and clean up later. It reads the problem early and steers.

Every living thing protects its inner environment this way: with information, in a closed loop, in real time. Environmental protection has had the rules for more than fifty years. What it has lacked is the loop. For the first time, the sensors, the glass threads, and the machines exist to build it for the environment outside.

> **Carry this.** Life has always protected its environment with information. Now we can build for the outer world what every body already has for the inner one.

*Status: the breathing reflex and its carbon dioxide sensors are established physiology. The comparison to environmental protection is synthesis, with a test: measure how long a facility takes to sense, decide, and act on a release, and compare that with how fast the release spreads.*

## What Is Established and What Is Open

Most of these exercises rest on measured or textbook physics. Where a line is an argument, its status says synthesis. The phrase “it is all ones and zeros” is true with three qualifiers.

1. **Finite: yes.** The exact state of any object can be written as a finite list of yes-or-no answers. This is established (exercise 10).
2. **Ones and zeros: almost.** At the smallest scale the unit is the quantum bit, which can hold a blend of yes and no until it is asked. Every answer nature gives back is a plain yes or no. This is established.
3. **Bottom layer: unknown.** That matter comes from information (John Wheeler’s “it from bit”) is a conjecture. No experiment has decided it. The it-bit view, that every thing is both at once, holds either way.
